01) 1. Describe reasons why so many (most?) companies are using cloud based email and calendar services

One major reason is cost and reduced IT maintenance. In the past, a company that wanted to operate its own email system would typically need servers, storage, backup systems, networking equipment and staff capable of maintaining them. The company would also have to deal with software updates and hardware failures. With a cloud service, much of this responsibility is transferred to the service provider. For example, instead of purchasing and maintaining an email server, a company can pay Microsoft or Google for user accounts and let the provider operate the infrastructure.

Another important reason is accessibility and flexibility. Cloud-based email and calendars can normally be accessed from almost anywhere with an internet connection. Employees can therefore use the same account from an office computer, laptop, tablet or smartphone. This is particularly useful for companies with employees working remotely or travelling. For example, an employee could receive an email on their laptop at work, check the same mailbox on their phone while travelling, and then use the company's calendar to join a meeting from home.

Cloud services also make collaboration easier. Calendars can be shared between employees, allowing people to see colleagues' availability and arrange meetings without repeatedly contacting everyone to find a suitable time. Services such as Microsoft 365 and Google Workspace also integrate email and calendars with other collaboration tools. A meeting invitation, for example, can automatically appear in an employee's calendar and contain a link to an online meeting.

Scalability is another significant advantage. A company can relatively easily add or remove user accounts as its workforce changes. A small business might start with ten employees and later grow to several hundred. With a cloud service, the organization does not necessarily need to purchase additional email servers simply because it has hired more employees. Instead, administrators can add accounts or change subscriptions as required.

    • 02) Compare few cloud data storage provider features and pricing (either for home users or for enterprises)

    There are several popular cloud data storage providers for home users, including Google One, Microsoft OneDrive and Dropbox. Google One gives every Google account 15 GB of free storage, which is shared between Google Drive, Gmail and Google Photos. Its Basic plan provides 100 GB of storage for about $1.99 per month. One useful feature is that the storage can be shared with up to five other people, making it suitable for families.

    Microsoft OneDrive is another popular option. Users receive 5 GB of free storage, while Microsoft 365 Personal provides 1 TB of OneDrive storage for one person. The main advantage of OneDrive is that it comes together with Microsoft applications such as Word, Excel, PowerPoint, Outlook and OneNote. It can also be used on computers, tablets and smartphones. This makes OneDrive particularly useful for students or people who already use Microsoft Office regularly.

    Dropbox provides 2 GB of free storage with its Basic plan. Its Plus plan provides 2 TB of storage for $9.99 per month when billed monthly, and it includes features such as file synchronization across devices and the ability to restore deleted files for 30 days. Dropbox is particularly focused on file storage, sharing and synchronization, so it can be a good choice for people who frequently work with documents and need to access the same files from different devices.

    Overall, the three providers have different advantages. Google One is a good choice for people who mainly use Google services and want inexpensive additional storage. OneDrive provides particularly good value for someone who also needs Microsoft Office applications, because the 1 TB storage is included with Microsoft 365 Personal. Dropbox provides considerably more storage in its Plus plan and focuses strongly on file synchronization and sharing. Therefore, I would choose Google One for simple personal storage, OneDrive for users who work with Microsoft Office, and Dropbox for users who need a large amount of storage and frequently share files. The prices can vary by country and can change over time, so the providers' official pricing pages should be checked before purchasing.

    • 03) Compare some large web hosting / web hotel provider offerings (not CDN)

    Large web hosting providers such as Hostinger and DreamHost allow individuals and companies to publish websites on the internet without having to purchase and manage their own physical servers. Hostinger offers packages that can include website hosting, SSD storage, SSL certificates, backups, email accounts and domain registration. DreamHost also provides website hosting, storage, SSL certificates, backups and support for multiple websites. One important difference between hosting providers is the amount of storage and the number of websites that can be hosted on each plan. Another important factor is the price. Hosting companies often advertise a low introductory price, but the price can increase when the subscription is renewed. For this reason, customers should compare the renewal price as well as the initial price. They should also consider backup systems, security, technical support, bandwidth and the technologies supported by the provider. For a small business, for example, using a web-hosting company is usually much easier than purchasing a server and having an employee maintain it.

    • 04) Your thoughts about when to maintain your own web server and when to use SaaS/PaaS/IaaS to provide web services?

    I think most small businesses and individuals should use cloud services or web hosting instead of maintaining their own physical web server. Operating your own server requires regular maintenance, security updates, backups and monitoring, and the company is also responsible if the hardware fails. For example, a small restaurant that only needs a website with its menu, contact information and opening hours would not normally need its own server because a cheap web-hosting service could provide everything required. However, maintaining your own server can be useful for organizations that need very specific hardware, software or security configurations and require complete control over their infrastructure. SaaS (Software as a Service) is suitable when a company simply wants to use an existing application, such as Microsoft 365 or Google Workspace, because the provider manages most of the technical infrastructure. PaaS (Platform as a Service) is useful for developers who want to build and run their own web applications without having to manage the underlying servers and operating systems. IaaS (Infrastructure as a Service) provides more control because the company can rent virtual servers, storage and networking resources and configure them according to its needs. Therefore, I would normally choose SaaS for standard business applications, PaaS for developing custom applications, and IaaS when more control over the infrastructure is required. A company should maintain its own physical web server mainly when there is a specific technical, security or business reason that cannot be handled effectively by a cloud or hosting provider.
